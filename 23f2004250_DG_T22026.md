### **1\. Abstract / Executive Summary**

This project solves the Smart MCQ Solver Challenge on Kaggle: given a question with five options (A to E), predict the top three most likely correct answers, scored by MAP@3.

I treated it as a binary classification problem. Each question was reshaped so every option became its own row with a "correct or not" label, letting me test four very different models on the same pipeline: LightGBM on engineered features, a from-scratch MLP on compressed TF-IDF embeddings, a fully fine-tuned RoBERTa transformer, and a LoRA-adapted version of RoBERTa.

The feature-based models won clearly. LightGBM scored 0.9928 MAP@3, the MLP 0.9896, fine-tuned RoBERTa 0.7650, and LoRA 0.6531. An ensemble of the top three models reached 0.9932, the best overall result. The key takeaway: option length and text-overlap patterns were strong, learnable signals in this dataset, and simpler models that could use them directly beat much larger models working from raw text with limited data.

### **2\. Introduction**

**Problem Statement.** Each question has five options, one correct. The task is to rank the top three most likely answers per question, scored with MAP@3.

**Objective.** Build and fairly compare at least three model types (a classical baseline, a from-scratch model, a fine-tuned pretrained model), add a bonus model, and combine the best ones into a final ensemble.

**Structure.** Section 3 covers the data and what I found in it. Section 4 covers text processing. Section 5 covers the models. Section 6 compares results. Section 7 wraps up.

### **3\. Dataset & Preprocessing**

The training set has 2,000 questions with five options each and one correct label; the test set has 500 questions in the same format, no answer column. No missing values.

Correct answers weren't evenly spread across positions: B was correct 24.5% of the time, C 22.95%, A 18.45%, D 17.9%, E 16.2%, against an even 20% baseline. I encoded this as a feature.

Text length mattered too. Prompts averaged 18 words; options averaged 26 words. Correct options ran longer on average (28.66 words) than incorrect ones (25.68 words), and the single longest option in a question was correct 34.95% of the time, well above the 20% random baseline. This was the strongest signal in the data and shaped most of my feature engineering.

Preprocessing was mainly the reshape described above: turning each five-option question into five labeled rows so every model solves the same "is this option correct" task. No data augmentation was used, since the existing signals were strong enough that augmentation risked diluting them.

### **4\. Tokenization Strategy**

For the transformer models, I used RoBERTa's own tokenizer (byte-level BPE), which handles rare words and typos gracefully by breaking them into subword pieces. RoBERTa itself is an improved version of BERT, trained on more data with a better masking strategy.

For the classical and from-scratch models, I used TF-IDF instead, since those models need numeric features rather than token sequences. I built one TF-IDF vectorizer (30,000 features, single words and pairs of words) across all prompt and option text, then used it two ways: cosine similarity between each option and its prompt as a direct feature, and a 64-dimension compressed version (via SVD) as input to the MLP. For RoBERTa, I tokenized each (prompt, option) pair together, truncated to 256 tokens.

I chose compressed TF-IDF over raw text for the MLP because the dataset is small, and dense, smaller inputs trained faster and generalized better than raw sparse text would have.

### **5\. Modeling & Experimentation**

All four models were evaluated using 2-fold GroupKFold cross-validation, grouped by question ID, so options from the same question never split across train and validation. This avoids the model seeing part of a question during training and being tested on the rest of it.

**Model 1: LightGBM (classical baseline).** A gradient-boosted decision tree model trained on the engineered features to classify each option as correct or not. Tree models suit this kind of tabular, non-linear data well and don't need much data to perform. After training, I printed feature importances averaged across folds, confirming that length and TF-IDF similarity features mattered most, matching the EDA findings. Settings: 31 leaves, learning rate 0.03, 2,000 max estimators with early stopping after 100 rounds without improvement.

**Model 2: From-scratch MLP.** A simple neural network built in PyTorch with no pretrained weights: two hidden layers (128 then 64 units) with ReLU activations and dropout, ending in a single output. Inputs were the compressed TF-IDF embeddings plus the same engineered features used by LightGBM, so the two models could be compared on equal footing. Trained with Adam (learning rate 1e-3) for up to 60 epochs, stopping early based on validation MAP@3 rather than loss. Best validation scores were 0.9932 and 0.9860 across the two folds.

**Model 3: Fine-tuned RoBERTa.** I fully fine-tuned all of RoBERTa-base's weights on (prompt, option) pairs, expecting the model's attention mechanism to capture meaning that surface features like length couldn't. Trained with AdamW (learning rate 2e-5) for 2 epochs, batch size 16\.

**Model 4 (bonus): LoRA-tuned RoBERTa.** Same backbone, but instead of updating all weights, I trained small adapter layers added to the attention layers (LoRA), keeping the rest of the model frozen. Only about 0.94% of the model's parameters were trainable. Trained for 3 epochs at a higher learning rate (1e-4), since only a small part of the model was learning.

**Ensemble.** I combined the three best-performing models (LightGBM, MLP, RoBERTa) as a weighted average based on each model's own score, excluding the weaker LoRA model, then re-ranked the combined predictions per question for the final submission.

### **6\. Performance & Comparative Analysis**

Models were scored on MAP@3 (the competition metric) and, at a 0.5 probability cutoff, precision, recall, F1, and accuracy.

| Model | MAP@3 | Precision | Recall | F1 | Accuracy |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| LightGBM | 0.9928 | 0.9918 | 0.9710 | 0.9813 | 0.9926 |
| MLP (from scratch) | 0.9896 | 0.9894 | 0.9330 | 0.9604 | 0.9846 |
| RoBERTa (fine-tuned) | 0.7650 | 0.8487 | 0.1430 | 0.2448 | 0.8235 |
| RoBERTa \+ LoRA | 0.6531 | 0.0000 | 0.0000 | 0.0000 | 0.8000 |
| **Ensemble** | **0.9932** | — | — | — | —  |

The gap between the tabular models and the transformers was the clearest finding here. LightGBM and the MLP both use the same engineered features, particularly length and overlap, and both scored near-perfectly. The transformers had to learn correctness cues from raw text with only 2,000 training questions, which is a small dataset for that kind of model. RoBERTa's full fine-tune managed a middling score, while LoRA, training under 1% of the parameters, couldn't adapt fast enough in the epoch budget given and ended up never predicting "correct" above the 0.5 cutoff, hence the zero precision and recall, even though its raw ranking still earned partial MAP@3 credit.

LightGBM was also far cheaper to train, seconds versus several minutes per fold for the transformers, so in this case the simpler, cheaper model was also the more accurate one. The ensemble improved marginally over LightGBM alone (0.9932 vs 0.9928), showing the other models added a small amount of useful signal despite scoring lower individually.

**Kaggle Performance.** The final selected submission scored **0.75394 on the public leaderboard** and **0.76094 on the private leaderboard**, placing **109th** in the final private standings (up 439 spots from the public rank). 

### **7\. Conclusion & Future Work**

The main lesson from this project was that the right model depends on the data, not on how advanced it sounds. I expected the transformer to win, but the exploratory analysis step turned out to matter more: spotting that option length predicted correctness let simpler models exploit that signal directly, while the transformers had to rediscover it with far less data than they'd normally need. I also got practical experience with how differently full fine-tuning and LoRA behave, especially around learning rate and training time.

The main challenge was getting LoRA to train well in a reasonable time, since it updates so few parameters it needed a higher learning rate and more epochs, and still underperformed. Keeping GroupKFold grouped by question ID also took care, to avoid leaking options from the same question across splits.

For future work, I'd give the transformers more training time and a learning rate warmup schedule, try a different pretrained model better suited to this kind of classification task, and explore combining the engineered features with transformer embeddings directly in one model instead of only at the ensemble stage. More cross-validation folds would also give a more reliable score estimate, at the cost of extra training time.
